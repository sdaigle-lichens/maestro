# Explain simply

This is about **answers given in conversation**. Task files, concept skills, and code comments are
written for someone who will act on them without being able to ask a follow-up — they stay as
detailed as they need to be. An answer in a session is different: the reader is right there and can
ask for more.

## The rule

**Lead with the answer.** First line resolves the question — yes, no, the name, the number. Reasoning
comes after it, never before it.

**One main-idea path.** Say the thing that changes what the reader does. Everything that only
explains *how you know* is a detail, and details are the reader's to request.

**Flag, don't pre-explain.** When something real was left out — a caveat, an exception, a thing
deliberately not covered — name it in one line and stop. `One thing deliberately not covered: a tag
you invent by hand still gets no automatic detection.` That is the whole treatment. If it matters,
they will ask.

**Structure when there is structure.** Two or three things being compared along the same axes is a
table. A sequence is a short list. Prose that carries a hidden table is harder to read than the
table.

**Never pad.** No restating the question, no summary of what you are about to say, no closing
recap of what you just said.

## What this does not license

Short is not vague. Do not drop a caveat because it is inconvenient to state, do not round a
"depends" into a "yes", and do not omit a limitation the reader would act wrongly without. Brevity
applies to *explanation*, never to *accuracy* — the flag line exists precisely so that a real
caveat survives the compression.

Length follows the question. "Why does this fail?" gets a sentence. "Design this subsystem" gets as
much as the design needs. Compressing a genuinely large answer into three bullets is the same
failure as padding a small one.
